Risk in Practice / Issue 02 essay

The turbine foundation survived its first winter. Why go back and measure?

From "looks fine" to verified effective: risk management lessons from an offshore wind farm

Author's analysis2026.11.18About 12 minutes

Translated from the author's Chinese original. 閱讀中文原文

Off England's south coast, after the wind and waves of its first winter, the English Channel gradually settles.

From a distance, row after row of offshore turbines still stand firm, blades turning in the sea breeze. Remote monitoring has flagged nothing major, and the turbines keep running normally.

Looking only at that, you might think: "Great, we got through the winter. The project must be a success, right?"

But below the surface, there is a test we can't see.

Offshore turbine foundations are worked on by currents and waves year after year, and the sand around them can gradually be carried away. This is called scour. If scour keeps developing, it can weaken the support around the foundation and even threaten the structure's long-term safety.

To tackle this, the UK's Rampion offshore wind farm brought in a rather unusual pilot around turbine J04.

The engineering team placed about 75,000 specially designed Reef Cubes, hoping these structures would slow scour while giving marine life places to attach, hide and live.

In other words, they wanted one piece of engineering to hit two targets at once:

Protect the turbine foundation, and open up more possibilities for marine life.

Initial survey results published in October 2026 brought good news. After the first winter, the cubes had broadly kept their intended layout with no significant breakage, and the early scour protection performance was encouraging.

But the story doesn't end there.

Because another important question can only be answered by further surveys:

Have these cubes actually made the marine ecosystem better?

Early engineering results can't prove that ecological benefits have been achieved. Even if fish are seen gathering in future, we'd still need to know whether that means new ecological productivity, or simply creatures that lived somewhere else moving here.

That's what makes this case most worth thinking about.

Getting through the first winter doesn't mean every problem is solved; the engineering looking stable doesn't mean every expected benefit has been proven.

1. Why do we keep treating "nothing happened" as "management works"?

Working in offshore wind operations and maintenance (O&M), I deal constantly with safety management documents, risk assessments, contractor work plans and corrective and preventive actions after incidents.

Over the years I've noticed something worth worrying about.

We're very good at proving "it's been done". We're not always as good at proving "what we did actually works".

Once an improvement is finished, the contractor submits photos, updates the risk assessment, completes training and attaches a sign-off record.

Every field is filled in, and the documents go through review by the book.

So the status in the system changes from "In progress" to "Completed".

The manager relaxes, the project closes, and the next job begins.

But I always want to ask one more question:

"How do we know this problem won't happen the same way again?"

That's not to say documents, training or procedures have no value.

Quite the opposite: for high-risk work, complete procedures, permits to work, risk assessments and records are indispensable management tools.

The problem is that once these tools become the management result in themselves, we can slip, without noticing, into mistaking "procedure completed" for "risk controlled".

I call this "bundled good news".

As soon as a project gets a positive result on one front, it's easy to wrap up its other, unverified goals and call them a success too.

For example:

  • Equipment installed without a hitch doesn't mean its safety functions have been fully verified.
  • Contractors completing training doesn't mean people on site can handle abnormal situations correctly.
  • A corrective action being signed off doesn't mean the risk factors behind the incident are effectively controlled.
  • Going a while without an accident doesn't mean the system has no weak points still waiting to show.

No accident is something to be glad about; but on its own it's not sufficient evidence that controls are working.

If we judge management quality only by whether accidents happen, in a sense we're waiting for risk to give us the answer in the most expensive way possible.

Mature risk management should be able to show evidence, before any accident, of whether key controls are doing their job.

2. Taking "success" apart: Rampion's three verification tracks

Back to the Rampion offshore wind farm.

What draws me to this case isn't just the innovative reef cubes. It's that it shows how one piece of engineering can pursue different goals at the same time, but each goal needs its own evidence; one can't stand in for another.

From a risk governance angle, we can sort it into three verification tracks.

TrackQuestion to answerCurrent status
A | Engineering performanceIs the protection actually working?Positive so farFirst survey shows the intended layout held through the first winter
B | Ecological benefitHas the change we hoped for really happened?Not enough evidenceEcological survey under way, no published results yet
C | Operational safetyHow much risk are we willing to take to get the answer?ConstraintSea conditions limit trips offshore; survey window extended to 1 Dec

Track A: Engineering performance. Is the protection actually working?

The first question is whether the reef cubes can keep providing the intended scour protection under currents and waves.

The engineering team needs to care about more than whether the cubes landed in the right place. It also needs to ask:

  • After winter storms, have any units moved unexpectedly or broken?
  • Has the surrounding seabed shown any unexpected scour?
  • Is the protection still performing the function the design requires?

The first survey provided positive evidence of engineering performance.

But what that evidence means should be stated precisely.

It shows the protection performed as expected over the period and conditions observed; it doesn't mean the long-term safety of the whole turbine foundation has now been fully proven.

After all, offshore turbines are often designed to last decades.

Getting through the first winter is an important milestone, but it's not the finish line for long-term reliability.

Track B: Ecological benefit. Has the change we hoped for really happened?

The cubes' other goal is to create habitat for marine life.

But a structure having gaps doesn't mean marine life will move in; and seeing creatures move in doesn't mean the ecosystem as a whole has improved.

So an ecological survey needs to look at more than "are there fish?" It may also cover species mix, how organisms attach, how the habitat is used, and changes over time.

More importantly, it needs a proper baseline for comparison.

If more fish turn up around the new reef, we have to think further:

Is that the effect of the reef design, or a change caused by the season, currents, differences in existing habitat, or other environmental factors?

Without baseline data or a proper comparison design, it's easy to mistake changes that happened at the same time for results of the improvement.

So for ecological verification that isn't finished, the most responsible thing to say is neither "success" nor "failure".

It's this:

There isn't enough evidence yet; it still needs verifying.

That line may not look very impressive, but it can be worth more than any report claiming 100% success.

Track C: Operational safety. How much risk are we willing to take to get the answer?

Offshore work has one very practical constraint: wanting to go to sea doesn't mean you can.

Wind speed, wave height, currents, visibility, vessel capability and personnel transfer conditions can all affect whether a survey at sea can be done safely.

When engineering or ecological monitoring runs into sea conditions unfit for work, managers have to decide between schedule, data needs and people's safety.

This track is different from the other two.

Engineering performance and ecological benefit are the results we want to verify; operational safety is the constraint we must respect while getting that evidence.

We can't push people on site into unnecessary risk just to meet a reporting deadline.

If the sea won't allow the survey, the right move is to reassess the weather window, adjust the plan and clearly record which data is still missing.

A delayed survey doesn't necessarily mean management has failed; forcing a survey through in unsafe conditions could create another, more serious problem.

These three tracks remind us:

A project can get positive results on engineering, still need more evidence on ecology, and adjust its survey schedule because of safety constraints, all at the same time.

They shouldn't be boiled down to one vague "success" or "failure".

3. From closing a CAPA to verifying effectiveness: what have we actually finished?

In HSE management, corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) after an incident investigation are where it's easiest to fall into the same trap.

Imagine this.

During an equipment maintenance job, it turns out a contractor technician didn't carry out lockout/tagout (LOTO) correctly as required.

After the investigation, the contractor puts forward an improvement plan:

Update the procedure, retrain, ask supervisors to step up oversight, and add check items before work starts.

A week later, everything is done.

The paperwork says: "CAPA Closed".

But is the problem really solved?

As I see it, at least three things still need telling apart.

  1. ImplementationDid we do it?
  2. EffectivenessDoes what we did actually work?
  3. Sustained performanceWill this improvement keep working?

Level 1: Implementation

Were the improvements carried out as planned?

For example: was the procedure updated, was the training completed, were the equipment or tools improved?

These are all necessary checks.

But all they answer is:

"Did we do it?"

Level 2: Effectiveness

Did the improvements actually achieve what they were meant to?

For example: can technicians carry out energy isolation correctly in different work situations? Is the isolation point information used on site clear? Are there design issues that make it easy to misjudge or skip a step?

If the improvement involves equipment interlocks or other engineering controls, their effectiveness should also be confirmed with suitable function tests.

What this stage has to answer is:

"Does what we did actually work?"

Level 3: Sustained performance

When the manager isn't on site, the contractor brings in a new crew, or the job goes into a rush phase, does the improvement still work?

This may need follow-up spot checks, field observation, function testing, trend analysis or other verification methods proportionate to the risk.

What this stage has to answer is:

"Will this improvement keep working?"

Three questions that look much the same, but in practice they stand for completely different depths of management.

And verifying effectiveness doesn't have to mean HSE staff turning up in person every time.

What really matters is that every key improvement has a clear owner, a suitable verification method, acceptable pass criteria, and the necessary follow-up.

For some measures, one function test can provide important evidence; for others that involve people's behaviour or long-term environmental change, it may take repeated observation or continuous monitoring.

Truly closing the loop isn't switching the system status to "Completed". It's having enough evidence to back the improvement we claim.

If a measure is done but its effectiveness is still to be confirmed, we should say so honestly, rather than letting both disappear into the same green tick.

4. The skill managers need most: admitting we don't know yet

To my mind, the truly hard part of management isn't producing an improvement plan that looks complete.

It's whether, when a manager, client or project team asks "Is this problem solved?", we can clearly explain:

  • What has been done?
  • Which results have evidence behind them?
  • Which risks still remain?
  • Which questions need more time or a different method to confirm?

In a high-pressure project, admitting "we don't know yet" isn't easy.

Because everyone wants the problem solved fast, the project closed smoothly and the KPIs looking good.

But if we dress up unconfirmed things as solved to make a report look good, the organisation may lose the drive to keep tracking the risk.

I'd argue the opposite: mature risk governance should let managers say plainly:

"This measure is done and initial tests meet the requirements, but its long-term effectiveness still needs tracking."

That isn't a lack of confidence.

It's respect for evidence, and the humility unknown risks call for.

A manager's value isn't in guaranteeing nothing will ever go wrong. It's in building a system that lets the organisation keep finding problems, verifying improvements, and reassessing risk when conditions change.

5. One success doesn't mean a method works forever

This idea doesn't only apply to turbines at sea or industrial sites.

I had a similar experience recently while spending time with my kids.

For a while, every bath time I had to ask again and again just to get my child to walk into the bathroom.

Reminding, hurrying, reasoning, even raising my voice: none of it worked very well.

Then one day, I tried something different.

I asked: "Do you want to hop into the bathroom as a little monkey or a little penguin today?"

To my surprise, my child happily picked one and hopped into the bathroom ready for a bath.

Another time, at bedtime, the kids were still bouncing around excitedly on the bed.

Instead of insisting they lie down right away, I suggested we listen to a story together. They slowly calmed down and finally fell asleep.

In that moment, I really felt I'd found the answer.

So I didn't need to keep pushing; just changing how we interacted made things go smoothly!

But a few days later, the same trick stopped working.

My child was worn out from playing all day and close to a meltdown, and whatever choice I offered, nothing seemed to calm him down.

In that moment I started doubting again:

"Strange, it worked so well a few days ago. Why is it useless today?"

Thinking it over later, I realised I'd fallen into the same trap.

I'd treated one success as proof that the method would always work.

But a child's behaviour can be shaped by many things at once: tiredness, mood, what happened that day, routine, and how parent and child interact.

One smooth bath time doesn't mean a method works in every situation; one meltdown doesn't mean earlier attempts were worthless.

What I need to watch isn't just whether the method worked, but:

In what situations is it more likely to work? And under what conditions might it fail?

That's actually the same line of thinking as scour protection for offshore turbines.

Of course, parenting isn't an engineering experiment, and children aren't machines to be controlled. We can't, and shouldn't, turn every moment with our kids into a performance test.

But the experience reminded me of one thing:

Don't rush to declare you've found the standard answer after one success; and don't write off everything you've tried after one setback.

Reflection that actually helps means understanding how a method relates to its context, rather than judging success or failure on a single result.

6. Save your time for what holds up under scrutiny

We live in an age that prizes results.

Projects must finish on time, improvements must close fast, performance must keep rising.

We're used to declaring success as soon as things start looking better.

But sometimes what deserves the most caution isn't the plan that obviously failed. It's the system that looks perfectly normal and that nobody keeps questioning.

Back to the English Channel.

After the storms, the sea is calm again.

From a distance, the turbines still stand firm, as if nothing had happened.

But a truly responsible engineering team knows that the calm in front of them is only what has been observed so far, not proof that every problem is solved.

So they still go back and measure.

They measure changes in the seabed to confirm the protection is still working, and observe marine life to verify whether the ecological benefits they hoped for have really happened.

Management is the same.

Finishing an improvement doesn't mean the improvement works; one accident-free period doesn't mean the next will be safe.

And what we need most may not be more polished close-out reports, but a management attitude that keeps asking questions.

When everyone says "it should be fine now", we're still willing to ask:

  • "Where's the evidence?"
  • "What hasn't been confirmed yet?"
  • "If the environment or conditions change, will this method still work?"

Management worth trusting isn't the kind that produces impressive results every time. It's the kind that can explain clearly what has been proven, what is still unknown, and how we plan to keep checking.

Because real peace of mind never comes from "it looks fine".

It comes from being able to find the problems that haven't caused an accident yet, before they do.
