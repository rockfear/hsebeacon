We've warned them a hundred times. Why do accidents still happen?

From "be careful" to "make the system hard to get wrong"

Translated from the author's Chinese original. 閱讀中文原文

The "be careful" we've repeated to death, and the accidents that keep happening anyway

Ever been here? A colleague keeps forgetting to switch off equipment, someone keeps skipping an important step, and the supervisor ends up saying it over and over: "Pay more attention next time!" The strange part is that even after all the reminders, the same mistake still happens.

In a factory or on a construction site, that problem can cost more than a delay. It can end in a real accident.

As an HSE person, you're forever drumming it in at toolbox talks, covering the notice board with every safety slogan going, and reminding people to "stay safe" on every walk-round. But all it takes is a freak gust on site or one second of distraction, and an accident we've seen before plays out again in much the same way, sometimes exactly the same way.

First you swear under your breath a hundred times, "We've briefed this rule hundreds of times and they're still getting it wrong!" Then you start questioning yourself: "After all this awareness work, why do accidents keep happening?" Management's first reaction is always that the crew were careless and didn't follow the SOP. But is that really a people problem, or is it the whole organisation's habitual inertia?

Why do we keep using training to solve problems that should be solved by engineering design?

The default path of traditional management

When there's an accident or a problem, the usual fix is to "add or revise a checklist", then schedule training, and hand out warnings or fines.

In a high-level management model this kind of "management by addition" looks perfect and perfectly reasonable. After all, more records mean HSE can show it has done its job, with the evidence to prove it.

Say you add a stack of new sign-off forms. On paper there's more to check and watch before the job starts, so it looks safer. In reality you're spreading the crew's attention even thinner: they have to spend more energy and focus completing forms before they can even start the actual work.

And you know it, I know it, a blind man could see it: in the end those forms become a box-ticking exercise, and we're back where we started. "Why does the same mistake keep happening?"

Applied on the front line, this head-in-the-clouds style of management just shifts systemic risk onto frontline workers' bodies and attention. To put it bluntly, it's gambling.

The Irish distillery's early struggle

Picture this: every day at work you haul barrel after barrel weighing tens of kilos out of a shipping container. They're stacked together, the space is tight, and you have to reach, bend and pull while keeping an eye on whether a barrel above might suddenly slide off. Even with full training, one slip could mean a serious injury.

This isn't industrial history. It's a workplace a well-known Irish distillery actually faced.

Midleton Distillery in the south of Ireland employs more than 240 people and is a major player in Irish whiskey production. Carrying on a long tradition of excellence and a devotion to craftsmanship, it handles large numbers of empty barrels delivered every day. The empty barrels arrive in containers stacked three high, with up to 208 barrels per container.

Two operators unloaded these barrels "by hand" onto a rotary unloader. The work carried several musculoskeletal risks: empty barrels weigh up to 60 kg and, if they shifted in transit, could fall and hit an operator; and because the barrels were stacked in layers, they had to be moved from the top down to reach the bottom ones. Repetitive, heavy work like that leads to back injuries, crush injuries and more over time.

Although Midleton treats employee safety and health as a top priority and aims for zero accidents, when these injuries appeared they still relied on organisational controls to reduce the risk: shift rotation, cutting stacks to two layers, more personal protective equipment and more training.

If we already know a job hurts people, why keep training them to carry more safely, instead of asking: can we make it so nobody has to carry at all?

Midleton turned to automation company Pilz for a way to automate barrel unloading. With no off-the-shelf solution, they decided to develop an innovative, untested approach combining robotics and artificial intelligence to automate the barrel handling process.

After three years of development, an autonomous mobile robot (AMR) system was developed, installed and commissioned, and went into full operation in summer 2024.

Safety management has an important principle: rather than asking people to keep dodging hazards, first ask whether the hazard can be removed, or people separated from it by equipment. That's what the HSE world calls the "hierarchy of controls".

  1. EliminationTake the hazard out of the work
  2. SubstitutionSwitch to a less hazardous method or material
  3. Engineering controlsRobotic unloading, light curtains and interlocks: where the distillery ended up
  4. Administrative controlsRotation, lower stacks, training: where the distillery started
  5. Personal protective equipmentThe last line, and the one most dependent on people

Let's look at how Midleton broke out of its existing mindset and moved up the hierarchy of controls.

Admit the limits of willpower (first principles)

The attention of the brain's prefrontal cortex is an extremely scarce resource. Fatigue, heat, the environment and time pressure all wreck alertness.

When you're doing a task on the front line, checks before work are absolutely necessary. But checks that are cluttered, long-winded and largely unnecessary make people tired and irritable, which in turn lowers their attention and alertness.

Hand "people's responsibility" over to "physical boundaries"

With automated equipment, Midleton removed the need for operators to handle heavy loads directly in normal unloading, and used engineering controls such as safety light curtains and interlocks to make it less likely that people come near the danger zone.

That doesn't mean every hazard is gone. It means work that leaned heavily on people's strength and alertness becomes a system that engineering design, equipment guarding and maintenance management control together.

Of course, automation isn't the end of risk management. Once robots take over manual handling, the focus shifts to the human-machine interface, equipment faults, maintenance and the reliability of safety systems. Truly successful engineering controls don't just make normal operation safer; they also have to stop people being exposed again when equipment fails or someone needs to step in.

Get frontline operators involved in design (avoid ivory-tower engineering)

Why do so many automated systems end up hated and abandoned after they're brought in? They may be perfect in another plant or process, but in yours they turn out to be a nightmare to use, because the engineers never considered on-site variables before bringing them in. Try involving frontline operators in trial runs and in how abnormal situations are handled (jams, emergency stops), take on board what the site says and find the best balance through discussion. That's how a new system, new equipment or a new way of working actually takes root.

Save your time and energy for what really matters

EU-OSHA published this case in May 2025, less than a year after the system went into full operation in summer 2024.

According to the case, the development cost of the AI recognition and robotic system was not high compared with the losses from health and safety hazards, worker injuries, lost working hours and insurance costs; from start-up to the period covered by the case, no barrel handling accidents were reported. That result is limited to this task and observation period, and is worth following up.

The system can unload barrels stacked three at a time, and the robot can be adjusted for different sizes or containers, which means it could be applied in more related industries and fields.

What really shows HSE professionalism isn't how many reminders you repeat or how many inspections you carry out. It's whether you have the eye and the mindset to "engineer or systematise" the chaos, break out of the loop, step up a level and find a more effective way to solve the problem.

When a problem can only be avoided by constant reminders, stop blaming the people doing the job for not trying hard enough. Step back and redesign your environment and tools. The strongest safety net has never been a slogan on the wall; it's mistake-proofing built right into the work.

This doesn't only apply to factories.

If colleagues keep forgetting to fill in important information, instead of reminding them every day, make the system refuse to submit incomplete records.

If someone keeps forgetting to switch off equipment, instead of putting up more warning signs, see whether you can design in auto shut-off or a safety interlock.

Good management doesn't expect everyone never to make mistakes. It makes sure a predictable slip doesn't easily turn into a serious outcome. That's the essence of risk management.
